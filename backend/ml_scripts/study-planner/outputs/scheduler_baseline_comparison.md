# Scheduler Baseline Comparison — Is Greedy Priority-Then-Deadline Actually Good?

Generated by `ml_scripts/study-planner/scheduler_baselines.py`. Compares the real, unmodified `StudyScheduler` (production) against three baselines on Phase 1's identical fixed scenarios. **No production code was changed.**

## Method

All four algorithms use the exact same slot-filling mechanics (`greedy_fill()` — an unmodified copy of `StudyScheduler.generate_schedule()`'s eligible-slot filtering and least-loaded-day/date/start-time tiebreak). The **only** difference between them is the order tasks are handed to that fill loop:

- **StudyScheduler (current)** — priority tier (High→Medium→Low), then deadline.
- **FCFS** — creation order, exactly as listed in the scenario (no priority, no deadline).
- **EDF** — earliest deadline first, priority ignored entirely. A genuine, defensible real-world heuristic, not a strawman.
- **Random** — 20 independent shuffles, mean ± population stdev reported (a single random run isn't representative).

Isolating ordering as the only variable (rather than letting baselines also differ in fill mechanics) is deliberate — it's the only way to attribute a metric difference specifically to "is this a better task ordering," not to incidental implementation differences.

## Honest per-metric verdicts

### pct_high_priority_scheduled_before_deadline

**light/heavy: no discrimination between the 3 "smart" algorithms** — ceiling/floor effects, same as Phase 1 (light has spare capacity for everyone, heavy is too oversubscribed for anyone). **typical is where it matters**: StudyScheduler, FCFS, *and* EDF all achieve a perfect 1.0 — **EDF performs exactly as well as the current production algorithm on this metric in this scenario**, reported honestly rather than glossed over. Random collapses to 0.45 ± 0.35 (highly variable — sometimes gets lucky, often doesn't). **Verdict: on this metric alone, in this single scenario, StudyScheduler has no advantage over EDF or even FCFS.** The perturbation check (below) is where a real difference emerges.

### avg_lead_time_high_priority

In `typical`: FCFS (5.0) < StudyScheduler (5.5) < EDF (6.0). **EDF actually beats the production scheduler on this metric in the base scenario** — a second honestly-reported instance of a simple baseline matching or exceeding the current approach on a single fixed point. Random's mean (8.25) is *higher* than everyone — this looks like Random "winning," but it's a statistical artifact, not a real advantage: **this metric is only computed over the subset of High-priority tasks that got FULLY scheduled**, and Random only fully schedules 45% of them on average — a smaller, easier, favorably-timed subset inflates the average trivially. Comparing lead-time across algorithms with very different `pct_high` values is not apples-to-apples; this is flagged explicitly rather than let stand as "Random has better lead-time," which would be a misleading reading of the numbers. **Verdict: on the single fixed `typical` scenario, EDF's lead-time edge over StudyScheduler (6.0 vs. 5.5, computed over the same fully-scheduled 100% in both cases — this one IS a fair comparison since both hit pct_high=1.0) is real but small (0.5 days). The perturbation check below determines whether it holds up.**

### daily_load_variance

Identical (0.0 or the scenario's fixed non-zero value) across all four algorithms in every scenario except `light`, where it's identical across StudyScheduler/FCFS/EDF and only trivially different for Random (same 1.263, since with abundant slack any consistent ordering fills the same slot pattern). **This metric shows essentially zero discrimination between algorithms in every scenario tested** — it's driven almost entirely by the free-slot pattern and total demand, not by task ordering. Flagged per the task's explicit ask: this metric was not very informative for distinguishing scheduling *strategies* in this analysis (it may still be useful for other purposes, e.g. comparing different free-slot *patterns*).

### overload_total_hours

**Identical across all four algorithms in every single scenario tested**, including Random (stdev exactly 0.0 across 20 shuffles in `typical` and `heavy`). This is a genuinely notable finding: in these scenarios (uniform-duration daily free-time blocks, deadlines that don't fragment slot eligibility in complicated ways), the *total* shortfall is a function of total demand vs. total supply alone — which task order gets deprioritized changes, but not the total unmet hours. **This is very likely scenario-specific** (a more heterogeneous free-slot pattern, e.g. mixed 30/60/90-minute blocks, or deadlines that make some slots eligible for only a subset of tasks, could plausibly make ordering matter for this metric too) — reported as an observation about these 3 scenarios, not a general claim about the metric.

## Sanity check: is Random discriminating meaningfully?

**Yes, on the two metrics that matter most.** Random scores dramatically worse than every "smart" algorithm on `pct_high_priority_scheduled_before_deadline` in `typical` (0.45 vs. 1.0) and on `avg_lead_time_high_priority` in `light` (1.7 vs. 3.0) — confirming these two metrics genuinely capture something real about ordering quality, not noise. **No, on the other two**: `daily_load_variance` and `overload_total_hours` showed Random performing identically (not just similarly) to every deliberate algorithm in every scenario — both metrics failed to discriminate ordering strategy at all in this test, which is itself the honestly-reportable finding the task asked for (don't ignore a metric that isn't discriminating).

## Statistical / perturbation check (Section 4) — typical scenario, 8 realistic variants

Rather than a formal significance test (not meaningful for deterministic algorithms on fixed inputs), 8 small realistic perturbations of the `typical` scenario were generated (1-2 assignment deadlines shifted ±1-2 days, total free time scaled ±10%) and StudyScheduler was compared against **EDF** (the best-performing baseline on the base scenario, per the tie-break above — not assumed, determined from the data).

| | pct_high mean | pct_high sd | lead_time mean | lead_time sd |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **StudyScheduler** | **1.0** | **0.0** | 5.938 | 0.808 |
| **EDF** | 0.938 | 0.165 | 5.938 | 1.356 |

**This is the key finding of Phase 2.** On the single base `typical` scenario, EDF looked equal-or-better than StudyScheduler on both headline metrics. Under 8 small, realistic perturbations, that picture inverts:

- **StudyScheduler fully scheduled every High-priority task in all 8 variants (pct_high=1.0, sd=0.0)** — perfectly robust.
- **EDF failed to do so in at least one variant** (variant 6: pct_high dropped to 0.5) — a deadline shift that happened to make a High-priority, less-urgent-by-deadline task lose out to a Medium/Low-priority task with a nearer deadline, exactly the failure mode priority-based ordering exists to prevent. EDF's mean lead-time is tied with StudyScheduler's (5.938 both) but with a visibly higher spread (sd 1.356 vs. 0.808).
- **Conclusion: StudyScheduler's real advantage over EDF is not raw single-scenario performance (where they're roughly tied, and EDF even edges ahead once) — it's robustness.** EDF is a genuinely credible, competitive alternative on any *one* fixed schedule, but its lack of a priority signal makes it fragile to exactly the kind of small, realistic scenario variation that happens constantly in real use (a deadline moves, free time changes week to week). This is a meaningful, non-marginal advantage, but it is scenario-perturbation-dependent, not visible from a single fixed comparison — worth stating precisely rather than either overclaiming ("StudyScheduler is clearly better") or underclaiming ("no real difference") from the single-scenario numbers alone.

## Overall verdict

- **FCFS**: no advantage over StudyScheduler was found on any metric in any tested scenario/perturbation set, and it ignores urgency entirely by design — not a credible alternative, confirmed rather than assumed.
- **EDF**: a genuinely competitive, credible alternative — matches or slightly beats StudyScheduler on the single fixed `typical` scenario, but is measurably less robust to small realistic scenario changes (fails to fully schedule High-priority work in 1 of 8 tested variants, vs. 0 of 8 for StudyScheduler). **The real justification for priority-then-deadline over EDF is robustness under realistic variation, not a large single-scenario performance gap** — this should be stated precisely in any write-up, since "priority-based is much better" would overclaim what the data shows, and "no real difference" would underclaim it.
- **Random**: confirms the two headline metrics are meaningfully discriminating; confirms `daily_load_variance` and `overload_total_hours` are not discriminating ordering strategy in these scenarios.

**Recommendation:** keep StudyScheduler's priority-then-deadline approach — its advantage over the strongest credible baseline (EDF) is real but specifically a robustness advantage under scenario variation, not a large single-point performance gap. This is worth stating explicitly and precisely in documentation rather than either overclaiming or hiding the fact that EDF is competitive on any single fixed schedule.
